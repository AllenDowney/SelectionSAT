The SAT Math Gap: Gender Difference or Selection Bias?

Male test takers score about 30 points higher than female test takers on the math section of the SAT. Does that reflect a difference in math ability, or a difference in who takes the test? If boys with weaker math skills are less likely to take the SAT than girls with the same skills, the observed gap overstates the difference in ability.

This site presents a Bayesian model that estimates how much of the gap selection can explain, using the score distributions the College Board published for 2011–2015. The code and data are on GitHub.

The talk¶

Presented at PyData Boston 2025 by Allen Downey.

  • Slides: Google Slides

  • Announcement and abstract: SAT math scores: gender difference or selection bias? (Probably Overthinking It, December 16, 2025)

  • The first notebook in the talk (the Poincaré problem, a simpler example of selection bias): bread.ipynb on Colab, from Think Bayes

Findings¶

  • Observed difference: in each year from 2011 to 2015, male test takers scored 31–34 points higher than female test takers.

  • Selection effects: the model estimates that boys with low math ability are less likely to take the SAT than girls with the same ability, and that selection is stronger for boys than for girls.

  • Difference in ability: after accounting for selection, the estimated difference in latent math ability is only about 5 points (posterior means of 507.5 for males and 502.1 for females).

So most of the observed gap could be explained by who takes the test rather than by a difference in ability.

On this site¶

  • The Model: the notebook presented in the talk, with the data, the model, a check that it recovers known parameters from simulated data, and the results. You can also run it on Colab.

  • Which Data Matter Most?: a leave-one-out analysis of which score bins most influence the fit.

  • Model Versions: the four versions of the model and how they differ.

Data¶

The data are the College Board’s “SAT Percentile Ranks for Males, Females, and Total Group” tables for college-bound seniors, mathematics section, 2011–2015, which give the number of male and female test takers at each score from 200 to 800. The README links the original reports.
